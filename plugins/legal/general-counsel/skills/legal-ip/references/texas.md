## Texas Uniform Trade Secrets Act (Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code ch. 134A) and limitations

- **Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 134A.002(6)(A)-(B)** · Information is a trade secret only if the owner took reasonable measures under the circumstances to keep it secret and it derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known or readily ascertainable through proper means. "the owner of the trade secret has taken reasonable measures under the circumstances to keep the information secret; and (B) the information derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known" https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/civil-practice-and-remedies-code/title-6/chapter-134a/section-134a-002/ (verified 2026-09-16; Justia 2025 Texas Statutes mirror and official CP.134A current through 89th Leg., 2nd C.S. (2025); added S.B. 953 eff. Sept. 1, 2013, last amended Acts 2017, ch. 37 (H.B. 1995) eff. Sept. 1, 2017) [VERIFIED]
- **Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 134A.004(a)-(b)** · Damages are actual loss plus unjust enrichment not counted in actual loss, or a reasonable royalty in lieu; on clear and convincing proof of willful and malicious misappropriation the fact finder may add exemplary damages capped at twice the subsection (a) award. "If willful and malicious misappropriation is proven by clear and convincing evidence, the fact finder may award exemplary damages in an amount not exceeding twice any award made under Subsection (a)." https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/civil-practice-and-remedies-code/title-6/chapter-134a/section-134a-004/ (verified 2026-09-16; Justia 2025 mirror and official CP.134A current through 89th Leg., 2nd C.S.; amended H.B. 1995 § 3 eff. Sept. 1, 2017, no later change; § 134A.002(1-a) defines clear and convincing) [VERIFIED]
- **Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 134A.007(a)-(b)** · The chapter displaces conflicting tort, restitutionary, and other Texas civil remedies for misappropriation of a trade secret; contractual remedies, civil remedies not based on misappropriation, and criminal remedies survive under (b)(1)-(3). "Except as provided by Subsection (b), this chapter displaces conflicting tort, restitutionary, and other law of this state providing civil remedies for misappropriation of a trade secret." https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/civil-practice-and-remedies-code/title-6/chapter-134a/section-134a-007/ (verified 2026-09-16; Justia 2025 mirror and official CP.134A current through 89th Leg., 2nd C.S.; (a)-(b) unchanged since the 2013 enactment; (c) repealed by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., 2nd C.S., ch. 7 (H.B. 16), § 12.10(19), eff. Dec. 4, 2025, which does not touch the displacement or savings text) [VERIFIED]
- **Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.010(a)-(b)** · Suit for misappropriation of trade secrets must be brought within three years after the misappropriation is discovered or by reasonable diligence should have been discovered; a continuing misappropriation is a single cause of action and limitations runs without regard to whether the act is single or continuing. "A person must bring suit for misappropriation of trade secrets not later than three years after the misappropriation is discovered or by the exercise of reasonable diligence should have been discovered." https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/civil-practice-and-remedies-code/title-2/subtitle-b/chapter-16/subchapter-a/section-16-010/ (verified 2026-09-16; Justia 2025 mirror; added Acts 1997, 75th Leg., ch. 26, § 1, eff. May 1, 1997, no amendment listed; deadline-rule: never compute a date from this line) [VERIFIED]
- **In re M-I L.L.C., No. 14-1045 (Tex. May 20, 2016) (orig. proceeding)** · TUTSA § 134A.006 gives the trial court discretion to exclude a competitor's designated representative from trade-secret portions of a temporary-injunction hearing, and refusing to do so without balancing the competing interests is an abuse of discretion; writ conditionally granted (Devine, J.). "We, therefore, conclude that the Act granted the trial court discretion to exclude Mezzatesta from portions of the temporary injunction hearing involving alleged trade secret information about which he was potentially unaware." https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/supreme-court/2016/14-1045.html (verified 2026-09-16; slip opinion read via Justia's mirror and the court-issued PDF through search.txcourts.gov docket 14-1045, no rehearing, case stored June 30, 2016; CourtListener reports 505 S.W.3d 569, published, 62 citing opinions, no reversal or vacatur; the balancing factors were later codified at § 134A.006(b) by H.B. 1995, eff. ## Sponsor angles

Each angle rests only on a VERIFIED bullet above. Mechanism, authority, the counterparty's best counter, and what defeats the counter.

1. **The trade-secret asset is only as good as the reasonable-measures record.** Mechanism: in diligence, a target's customer lists, pricing data, and process know-how carry trade-secret value only where NDAs, access controls, and marking exist and can be produced; the sponsor prices the asset down, or conditions closing on a remediation covenant, where the record is thin. Authority: § 134A.002(6)(A)-(B). Counter: the seller says industry practice is looser and the information is secret in fact. Defeat: both prongs are conjunctive in the text; secrecy in fact without reasonable measures is not a trade secret, and the sponsor holds the burden shift at signing, not at trial.
2. **Worst-case misappropriation exposure prices at three times compensatory.** Mechanism: a portfolio company accused of misappropriation (a hired competitor's executive, an acquired data set) faces actual loss plus unjust enrichment, or a royalty, and up to twice that again only on clear and convincing proof of willful and malicious conduct. Authority: § 134A.004(a)-(b). Counter: the plaintiff pleads willfulness from the hiring itself. Defeat: clear-and-convincing is the statute's own standard; a documented clean-room onboarding (no devices, written certification of non-retention, walled assignments) is the evidence that holds the multiplier at one.
3. **Preemption strips the plaintiff's tort scaffolding.** Mechanism: conversion, unjust enrichment, unfair competition, and civil conspiracy pleaded alongside a misappropriation theory are displaced; the sponsor moves on the pleadings and narrows the case to TUTSA plus contract. Authority: § 134A.007(a)-(b). Counter: the plaintiff argues its tort claims rest on facts other than misappropriation. Defeat: (a) displaces "conflicting tort, restitutionary, and other law" providing civil remedies for misappropriation; the only claims (b) saves are contractual, non-misappropriation civil, and criminal, so the plaintiff must show a distinct factual basis or lose the count.
4. **The NDA is the sponsor's own preserved vehicle.** Mechanism: because (b)(1) saves contractual remedies, the sponsor sues a departing executive or a counterparty on the NDA for breach whether or not the information clears the trade-secret definition, and stacks TUTSA for the exemplary tier. Authority: § 134A.007(b)(1); § 134A.002(6). Counter: the defendant says the NDA claim is a misappropriation claim in disguise. Defeat: the savings clause is express; a breach count needs a contract term and a breach, not a trade secret.
5. **Three-year discovery clock, no restart.** Mechanism: in diligence, a target's known-but-unsued misappropriation older than three years from discovery is barred as the target's claim and as a third party's claim against the target; the continuing-misappropriation rule means the counterparty cannot revive it by pointing to ongoing use. Authority: § 16.010(a)-(b). Counter: the claimant pleads late discovery under the reasonable-diligence prong. Defeat: (b) makes continuing misappropriation a single cause of action running from the first discovery; the sponsor's diligence memo dates the target's actual knowledge from board minutes and email. Deadline-rule: the day count is confirmed from statute text at use, never from this file.
6. **Anti-SLAPP is off the table for the departing-executive suit.** Mechanism: a portfolio company suing a former officer, employee, or contractor for trade-secret theft, usurped corporate opportunity, non-disparagement breach, or non-compete breach faces no TCPA motion, no early fee exposure, and no automatic discovery stay. Authority: § 27.010(a)(5)(A)-(B). Counter: the defendant characterizes the suit as retaliation for speech and argues the claim does not "arise from" the relationship. Defeat: the exemption is keyed to the relationship and the relief sought, both on the face of the petition; plead the relationship and the recovery theory in the first paragraph.
7. **The 60-day TCPA motion as the portfolio company's shield.** Mechanism: a defamation, business-disparagement, or tortious-interference suit against a portfolio company (a competitor upset by a comparative claim, a vendor upset by a review) meets a motion to dismiss filed inside 60 days of service that suspends all discovery until the court rules. Authority: § 27.003(b)-(c); § 27.006(b). Counter: the plaintiff seeks limited discovery under § 27.006(b) on good cause. Defeat: (b) discovery must be "specified and limited" and relevant to the motion; oppose anything broader, and confirm the 60-day count and any extension from the statute text at use.
8. **TDPSA is a cure regime, not a damages regime.** Mechanism: the Attorney General must send written notice at least 30 days before suit; a portfolio company that cures inside the window and delivers the written statement (cured, consumers notified, documentation, policy changes) takes the suit off the table; exposure otherwise is a per-violation civil penalty with no private right reported. Authority: §§ 541.154, 541.155(a)-(c). Counter: the AG counts violations per consumer, or later alleges breach of the cure statement, which is itself actionable under § 541.155(a). Defeat: draft the cure statement to the four statutory elements and nothing more, keep the documentation, and treat the statement as a covenant the company must keep; the per-consumer question is undecided on the verified record.
9. **TRAIGA compliance is a statutory defense, not a cost center.** Mechanism: every AI-enabled portfolio company deploying in Texas since January 1, 2026 sits under an AG-only regime with a 60-day notice-and-cure gate, a rebuttable presumption of reasonable care, and safe harbors for substantial NIST AI RMF compliance and red-team discovery; adopting and documenting the framework converts the $80,000 to $200,000 "uncurable" tier into the curable tier or no penalty at all. 13. **A royalty-based damages model must reproduce the structure of the comparable licence.** Mechanism: whether pricing a target's misappropriation exposure or a portfolio company's recovery, an expert who averages a sliding-scale comparable into a flat percentage produces no evidence of the full amount; the sponsor attacks the opposing model on legal sufficiency and builds its own from the comparable's actual terms. Defeat: the Court tolerates uncertainty only where no objective evidence exists; where a comparable licence is in the record the plaintiff must produce a model that tracks it, and the remedy for the flat-rate model was a new trial, not rendition.
14. **Accrual dates from commercial use, and diligence is a fact question.** Mechanism: a target's claim, or a claim against the target, accrues when the secret is put to commercial use for profit, and the three-year clock runs from when the owner knew or by reasonable diligence should have known of that use; the diligence memo dates actual knowledge from emails, board records, and market signals, because a limitations summary judgment needs conclusive proof of the discovery date. Authority: Berry-Helfand, Part II.B; § 16.010(a)-(b). Counter: the defendant argues acquisition alone started the clock. Defeat: the Court's rule is use, meaning commercial use by which the offending party seeks to profit, and SEPCO lost its limitations defense because it did not conclusively prove the owner knew or could have discovered use before the critical date. Deadline-rule: the day count is confirmed from statute text at use, never from this file.

Authority: Title Source, Inc. v. HouseCanary, Inc., 612 S.W.3d 517 (Tex. App., San Antonio 2020, pet. denied). Counter: the plaintiff says broad-form submission is the Texas default. Defeat: broad form holds only where every theory submitted is valid; the court of appeals held that commingling TUTSA-preempted fraud allegations, and unsupported misappropriation theories, required a new trial, and the Supreme Court of Texas denied review.
