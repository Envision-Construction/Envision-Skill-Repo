## Delaware

### Controller doctrine, SB 21, and remedies

- **Kahn v. M&F Worldwide Corp., 88 A.3d 635, 644 (Del. 2014) (No. 334, 2013)** · Del. Supreme Court, Mar. 14, 2014. A controller freeze-out merger gets business judgment review only when conditioned ab initio on both approval by an independent, adequately empowered special committee that satisfies its duty of care and an uncoerced, informed majority-of-the-minority vote. Sponsor as defendant (controller taking a portfolio company private): both devices from the first proposal or the common-law route closes. "We hold that business judgment is the standard of review that should govern mergers between a controlling stockholder and its corporate subsidiary, where the merger is conditioned ab initio upon both the approval of an independent, adequately-empowered Special Committee" https://law.justia.com/cases/delaware/supreme-court/2014/334-2013.html (verified 2026-09-16; Justia mirror of the 2014 slip opinion; quoted as controlling in Rutledge v. Clearway (Del. Feb. 27, 2026); the SB 21 synopsis preserves the common-law route beside § 144(c); A.3d pinpoint 644 not displayed on Justia) [VERIFIED]
- **In re Match Group, Inc. Derivative Litig., 315 A.3d 446 (Del. 2024) (No. 368, 2022), Seitz, C.J., introduction** · Del. Supreme Court, Apr. 4, 2024. Where a controller stands on both sides and receives a non-ratable benefit, entire fairness is presumptive; one MFW device shifts the burden only, and all MFW requirements are needed for business judgment review. Sponsor as defendant (spin-offs, intercompany deals, recapitalizations). "But the use of just one of these procedural devices does not change the standard of review. If the controlling stockholder wants to secure the benefits of business judgment review, it must follow all MFW's requirements." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=362250 (verified 2026-09-16; opinion PDF; the rule governs only proceedings pending on or before Feb. 17, 2025; superseding construction: Rutledge (Traynor, J.), https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=392120, states § 144(b) "displaced our ruling in Match": for proceedings commenced after Feb. 17, 2025 a non-going-private controller transaction is shielded by either disinterested-committee approval or a conditioned disinterested-stockholder vote, and § 144(c) keeps the dual requirement for going-private deals) [SUPERSEDED: Del. Code Ann. tit. 8, § 144(b)-(c) (85 Del. Laws c. 6, § 1, eff. Mar. 25, 2025), as construed in Rutledge v. Clearway Energy Group LLC, No. 248, 2025 (Del. Feb. 27, 2026)]
- **S.S. 1 for S.B. 21, 153d Gen. Assemb., 85 Del. Laws c. 6, § 3 (Del. 2025), bill detail and synopsis** · Delaware General Assembly; signed and effective Mar. 25, 2025. Applies to acts and transactions whenever occurring, but not to proceedings pending or § 220 demands made on or before Feb. 17, 2025. Sponsor as defendant: controller deals closed before March 2025 take the safe harbor unless already in litigation by the cutoff. "do not apply to or affect any action or proceeding commenced in a court of competent jurisdiction that is completed or pending, or any demand to inspect books and records made, on or before February 17, 2025." https://legis.delaware.gov/BillDetail/141930 (verified 2026-09-16; Senate 20-0 on 3/13/25, House 32-7 on 3/25/25 with HA 2-5 and HA 7 defeated and HA 1 and HA 6 stricken; constitutionality upheld in Rutledge v. Clearway) [VERIFIED]
- **Del. Code Ann. tit. 8, § 144(b) (85 Del. Laws c. 6, § 1); S.S. 1 for S.B. 21 synopsis § 1** · eff. Mar. 25, 2025. A controlling stockholder transaction that is not a going-private transaction is shielded from equitable relief and damages if approved by a committee of two or more directors each determined disinterested by the board, acting in good faith without gross negligence, or if conditioned on and approved by an informed, uncoerced majority of votes cast by disinterested stockholders, or if fair. Sponsor as defendant (affiliate deals, intercompany financings, related-party contracts): one cleansing device is enough. "a controlling stockholder transaction that does not constitute a “going private transaction” may be entitled to the statutory safe harbor protection if it is negotiated and approved or recommended, as applicable, by a majority of the disinterested directors then serving" https://legis.delaware.gov/BillDetail/141930 (verified 2026-09-16; synopsis quote verbatim; delcode § 144(b) text confirmed, history ends 85 Del. Laws c. 6 § 1, current through Aug. 27, 2026; upheld in Rutledge) [VERIFIED]
- **Del. Code Ann. tit. 8, § 144(c), (e)(6) (85 Del. Laws c. 6, § 1); S.S. 1 for S.B. 21 synopsis § 1** · eff. Mar. 25, 2025. A going-private transaction with a controller obtains the safe harbor only with both disinterested committee approval and a conditioned majority-of-the-minority vote, or if fair to the corporation and its stockholders; (e)(6) defines going-private as a Rule 13e-3 transaction for Exchange Act companies and otherwise any controller transaction cancelling substantially all disinterested holders' shares. Sponsor as defendant: take-privates of controlled portfolio companies still need both devices. "that constitutes a “going private transaction” may be entitled to the statutory safe harbor protection if it is negotiated and approved or recommended, as applicable, by a majority of the disinterested directors then serving on the committee and is conditioned" https://legis.delaware.gov/BillDetail/141930 (verified 2026-09-16; delcode § 144(c), (e)(6) text confirmed; Rutledge n.31 confirms; no amendment since 85 Del. Laws c. 6) [VERIFIED]
- **Del. Code Ann. tit. 8, § 144(e)(2), (d)(4) (85 Del. Laws c. 6, § 1)** · eff. Mar. 25, 2025. A controlling stockholder is one holding majority voting power, contractual power to elect a board majority, or functionally equivalent power through at least one-third of voting power; (d)(4) provides no one is a controller unless the definition is met. Sponsor as defendant: below one-third and without a contractual board-majority right, the statutory controller label does not attach. "by virtue of ownership or control of at least ⅓ in voting power of the outstanding stock of the corporation entitled to vote generally in the election of directors" https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc04/index.html (verified 2026-09-16; delcode current through acts enacted as of Aug. 27, 2026; source note ends 85 Del. Laws c. 6 § 1; HB 353 (2026) touched §§ 242, 275, 312 only) [VERIFIED]
- **Del. Code Ann. tit. 8, § 144(d)(5), (d)(6)c (85 Del. Laws c. 6, § 1)** · eff. Mar. 25, 2025. Controllers and control-group members are not liable for monetary damages for breach of fiduciary duty except for loyalty breaches, bad faith or knowing misconduct, or improper personal benefit; (d)(6)c preserves claims that a stockholder knowingly aided and abetted a director's breach. Sponsor as defendant: care claims are statutorily exculpated; loyalty and knowing aiding-and-abetting exposure remains. "No person who is a controlling stockholder or member of a control group shall be liable in such capacity to the corporation or its stockholders for monetary damages for breach of fiduciary duty other than for" https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc04/index.html (verified 2026-09-16; delcode current through Aug. 27, 2026; no amendment after 85 Del. Laws c. 6; Rutledge rejected the constitutional challenge, no injunction) [VERIFIED]
- **Rutledge v. Clearway Energy Group LLC, No. 248, 2025 (Del. Feb. 27, 2026), Traynor, J., introduction** · Del. Supreme Court, en banc, Feb. 27, 2026. Both certified questions answered in the negative: the § 144 safe harbors do not divest the Court of Chancery of equity jurisdiction and retroactive application does not violate Article I, § 9. Sponsor as defendant: the statutory controller regime is settled; the $117 million committee-approved Clearway asset purchase returns to Chancery under amended § 144. "the Safe Harbor Provisions set forth in Section 1 of SB 21 do not conflict with Article IV, § 10 of the Delaware Constitution." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=392120 (verified 2026-09-16; slip opinion; no reargument, rehearing, or certiorari found on courts.delaware.gov, CourtListener, or the State Court Report tracker) [VERIFIED]
- **In re Tesla, Inc. Derivative Litig., Nos. 534, 2024; 10, 2025; 12, 2025 (Del. Dec. 19, 2025) (per curiam), introduction** · Del. Supreme Court, en banc, Dec. 19, 2025. Rescission of the 2018 grant reversed as an improper remedy; plan reinstated; nominal damages awarded; counsel fees reset to quantum meruit; control, fairness, and ratification rulings left unreviewed. Sponsor as defendant (executive or sponsor equity awards): total rescission of fully performed awards is disfavored, so plaintiffs must build a record for partial rescission or disgorgement. "we reverse the remedy chosen by the Court of Chancery – rescission of the 2018 compensation plan. We reinstate the 2018 plan and award the Plaintiff nominal damages." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=389200 (verified 2026-09-16; per curiam PDF, affirmed in part, reversed in part; court of last resort; 2026 Chancery opinions cite it as controlling) [VERIFIED]
- **In re Cornerstone Therapeutics Inc. S'holder Litig., Nos. 564, 2014 & 706, 2014 (Del. May 14, 2015), Strine, C.J., Part I** · Del. Supreme Court, May 14, 2015. A plaintiff seeking only money damages must plead a non-exculpated claim against each director protected by a § 102(b)(7) charter provision to survive dismissal, even where entire fairness governs a controller squeeze-out. Sponsor as defendant: independent directors on a sponsor-controlled board exit early; the sponsor and its designees remain as the loyalty defendants. "A plaintiff seeking only monetary damages must plead nonexculpated claims against a director who is protected by an exculpatory charter provision to survive a motion to dismiss, regardless of the underlying standard of review for the board‘s conduct" https://courts.delaware.gov/opinions/download.aspx?ID=223540 (verified 2026-09-16; slip opinion; cited as authority in Rutledge (Del. 2026); amended § 144 did not alter the § 102(b)(7) pleading rule; "No. 564, 2014" not visible in the extracted caption) [VERIFIED]
- **Maffei v. Palkon, 339 A.3d 705 (Del. 2025) (No. 125, 2024), Valihura, J., conclusion** · Del. Supreme Court, Feb. 4, 2025. A board's decision to convert from Delaware to Nevada is reviewed under the business judgment rule absent well-pled allegations of a material, non-ratable benefit to directors or controllers; reduced future litigation exposure is too speculative to qualify. Sponsor as defendant (sponsor-controlled board redomesticating): business judgment protection when no litigation is pending or threatened. "For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the business judgment rule applies to the present case. Accordingly, we REVERSE the judgment below." https://law.justia.com/cases/delaware/supreme-court/2025/125-2024.html (verified 2026-09-16; Justia mirror of the slip opinion; Rutledge (Del. 2026) n.13 cites Maffei v. Palkon, 339 A.3d 705, 729 (Del. 2025) for the business-judgment presumption and n.15 cites Maffei, 339 A.3d at 730; reporter cite confirmed from the Rutledge slip opinion, courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=392120; no negative history) [VERIFIED]

### Deal litigation: aiding and abetting, appraisal, MAE, busted deals, earnouts

- **In re Mindbody, Inc., S'holder Litig., No. 484, 2023 (Del. Dec. 2, 2024), Valihura, J., introduction** · Del. Supreme Court, Dec. 2, 2024. Reversed the Chancery's holding that Vista aided and abetted the CEO's disclosure breach despite Vista's contractual proxy-review right and duty to flag known omissions. Sponsor as defendant (buyer): no liability for the target's omissions absent knowing participation. "Third, we reverse the trial court’s holding that Vista aided and abetted Stollmeyer’s disclosure breach." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=372380 (verified 2026-09-16; opinion PDF, affirmed in part, reversed in part; reporter cite 332 A.3d 349; SB 21 synopsis cites it for knowing participation; § 144(d)(6)c preserves the claim) [VERIFIED]
- **DFC Global Corp. v. Muirfield Value Partners, L.P., No. 518, 2016 (Del. Aug. 1, 2017), Strine, C.J., introduction** · Del. Supreme Court, Aug. 1, 2017. No judicial presumption for deal price, but where the sale came from a robust, arm's-length, conflict-free market check, deal price is the best evidence of fair value, and a financial buyer's IRR focus is no reason to discount it. Sponsor as appraisal respondent (buyer): run an open process and appraisal arbitrage loses its edge. "Any rational purchaser of a business should have a targeted rate of return that justifies the substantial risks and costs of buying a business. That is true for both strategic and financial buyers." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=260240 (verified 2026-09-16; slip opinion, reversed and remanded; 54 citing opinions on CourtListener, none negative) [VERIFIED]
- **Akorn, Inc. v. Fresenius Kabi AG, No. 535, 2018 (Del. Dec. 7, 2018) (order), Strine, C.J.** · Del. Supreme Court, en banc, Dec. 7, 2018. Summarily affirmed that Akorn suffered an MAE under § 6.02(c) excusing Fresenius from closing and that Fresenius validly terminated under § 7.01(c)(i) for regulatory representation breaches without itself being in material breach. Sponsor as defendant (buyer invoking a walk right): an MAE walk needs a durationally significant collapse and a clean record of the buyer's own covenant compliance. "that Akorn had suffered a material adverse effect (“MAE”) under § 6.02(c) of the Merger Agreement that excused any obligation on Fresenius’s part to close." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=282110 (verified 2026-09-16; three-page order affirming 2018 WL 4719347 (Del. Ch. Oct. 1, 2018); ordinary-course covenant holding not addressed; cited as authority in Surteco v. AIG Specialty (Del. Super. July 6, 2026)) [VERIFIED]
- **AB Stable VIII LLC v. MAPS Hotels & Resorts One LLC, No. 71, 2021 (Del. Dec. 8, 2021), Seitz, C.J., introduction** · Del. Supreme Court, en banc, Dec. 8, 2021. A seller's drastic pandemic-response changes made without buyer consent breached an unqualified ordinary-course covenant and excused the buyer from closing a $5.8 billion sale, regardless of industry-consistent conduct. Sponsor as seller (defendant): seek buyer consent before crisis operating changes; sponsor as buyer: the walk right is independent of the MAE allocation. "The court concluded correctly that the Seller’s drastic changes to its hotel operations in response to the COVID-19 pandemic without first obtaining the Buyer’s consent breached the ordinary course covenant and excused the Buyer from closing." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=327380 (verified 2026-09-16; slip opinion affirming 2020 WL 7024929; title-insurance condition not reached; relied on in Surteco (Del. Super. 2026)) [VERIFIED]
- **Snow Phipps Group, LLC v. KCAKE Acquisition, Inc., C.A. No. 2020-0282-KSJM (Del. Ch. Apr. 30, 2021), McCormick, V.C., introduction** · Court of Chancery, post-trial, Apr. 30, 2021. Kohlberg's affiliates ordered to close the $550 million DecoPac acquisition: no MAE, no ordinary-course breach, and the debt-financing condition to specific performance deemed met under the prevention doctrine because the buyers breached their reasonable-best-efforts financing obligation. Sponsor as defendant (buyer): a financing-out fails after the buyer manufactures the financing failure. "Applying the prevention doctrine, this decision deems the debt financing condition met because the buyers contributed materially to lack of debt financing by breaching their reasonable-best-efforts obligation." https://courts.delaware.gov/Opinions/Download.aspx?id=319750 (verified 2026-09-16; memorandum opinion; no Supreme Court decision in the case; 51 citing opinions treat it as authority; author line absent from extracted text, KSJM suffix only) [VERIFIED]
- **Johnson & Johnson v. Fortis Advisors LLC, No. 490, 2024 (Del. Jan. 12, 2026), LeGrow, J., introduction** · Del. Supreme Court, en banc, Jan. 12, 2026. Implied-covenant holding reversed because the merger agreement expressly tied regulatory milestones to 510(k) clearance and allocated FDA pathway risk to sellers; breach of the express efforts clause for later milestones and the damages methodology affirmed. Sponsor as seller-side plaintiff (earnout): draft milestones to cover alternative regulatory pathways; the implied covenant will not rewrite a foreseeable allocation. The Merger Agreement repeatedly and expressly conditioned the regulatory earnouts on obtaining 510(k) premarket notification" https://courts.delaware.gov/opinions/download.aspx?id=389980 (verified 2026-09-16; slip opinion, affirmed in part, reversed in part, remanded for recalculation excluding Milestone 1; no reargument found) [VERIFIED]

### Stockholder agreements and sponsor governance rights

## Sponsor angles

Each angle rests only on VERIFIED bullets above; the counterparty's conservative reading is stated as its position, with the counter.
1. Non-going-private controller deals need one cleansing device. Mechanism: route sponsor-affiliate financings, intercompany contracts, and recapitalizations through a committee of two or more directors each determined disinterested by the board, acting in good faith without gross negligence. Authority: § 144(b), SB 21 § 3 cutoff, Rutledge. Counterparty's best counter: plead that a committee member is interested, that the board's disinterestedness determination was made in bad faith, or that the deal cancels substantially all disinterested holders' shares and is therefore going-private under § 144(e)(6)b. What defeats it: board minutes recording the disinterestedness determination for each member, a committee of at least two, and a structure that leaves disinterested holders' shares outstanding.
2. Stay outside the statutory controller definition. Mechanism: hold under one-third voting power and take no contractual right to elect a board majority. Authority: § 144(e)(2), (d)(4). Counter: plaintiff pleads a proceeding pending on or before Feb. 17, 2025 (SB 21 § 3), or that designee and consent rights amount to power to elect a majority. What defeats it: designee rights capped below a majority and a closing date after the cutoff.
3. Take-privates run MFW from the first proposal. Mechanism: condition the initial proposal on both an independent committee and a majority-of-the-minority vote. Authority: MFW; § 144(c). Counter: a committee member with sponsor ties, or a coerced or uninformed vote. What defeats it: independence diligence on every committee member before the first letter, and disclosure that survives a Mindbody-style omission attack.
4. Legacy stockholder agreements: laches from execution plus § 122(18). Mechanism: defend consent and veto rights as voidable-not-void and time-barred three years after execution; for post-August 2024 agreements, recite board-approved minimum consideration. Authority: Moelis; SB 313 § 6; § 122(18). Counter: an as-applied challenge to a specific exercise of the right, or an action pending before Aug. 1, 2024. What defeats it: exercise the rights on a record showing the board acted consistently with the agreement, and paper the minimum consideration.
5. Redomestication under business judgment. Mechanism: convert the portfolio company to Nevada or Texas before any claim is pending or threatened. Authority: Maffei. Counter: plaintiff pleads a material, non-ratable benefit to directors or the sponsor from reduced exposure to an identified pending claim. What defeats it: a board record showing no pending or threatened litigation at the vote.
6. Appraisal: deal price is fair value after an open process. Mechanism: run a conflict-free, arm's-length market check and defend deal price. Authority: DFC Global. Counter: petitioner attacks the process for conflicts or MBO features. What defeats it: pre- and post-signing market checks documented in the proxy and no management rollover negotiated before the check.
7. Busted deal, sponsor as buyer. Mechanism: walk only on a durationally significant collapse (Akorn) or an unconsented drastic operating change (AB Stable), never on a financing failure the buyer caused (Snow Phipps). Counter: seller invokes the prevention doctrine and seeks specific performance. What defeats it: a contemporaneous reasonable-best-efforts financing record and written consent requests on every crisis operating change.
8. Earnouts, sponsor as seller. Mechanism: draft milestones that cover alternative regulatory pathways and preserve fraud claims by resisting one-way anti-reliance. Authority: J&J v. Fortis. Counter: buyer ties the milestone to the single pathway named in the agreement. What defeats it: pathway-neutral milestone definitions and an express efforts clause with a priority covenant.
9. Aiding and abetting, sponsor as buyer. Mechanism: exercise the contractual proxy-review right without steering the target's disclosure. Authority: Mindbody; § 144(d)(6)c. Counter: plaintiff pleads knowing participation in a known omission. What defeats it: a review record showing no knowledge of the omitted fact.
10. Early exit for independent directors. Mechanism: a § 102(b)(7) charter provision. Authority: Cornerstone. Counter: plaintiff pleads a loyalty or bad-faith claim against each director individually. What defeats it: no individual conflict on the record for the independents.
11. LBO consideration through the depositary. Mechanism: pay every dollar of LBO or recap consideration, including management and employee shares, through the paying agent or depositary and document the customer relationship. Authority: Merit; Tribune; Nine West. Counter: trustee pleads that side payments ran outside the conduit, and defendants bear the § 546(e) burden. What defeats it: no payroll-system or side-channel payments.
12. Uptier drafting. Mechanism: use "purchase by way of assignment ... at any time" without an "open market" qualifier, or an express "privately negotiated" clause, and keep each step compliant with the indenture text. Authority: Mitel; Wesco (S.D. Tex.); against, Serta and its remand. Counter: excluded lenders sue under § 2.18(c) ratable treatment and seek damages from participating lenders. What defeats it: document text that permits non-pro-rata exchanges expressly; a Fifth Circuit venue with Serta language loses.
13. Sponsor tortious-interference defense in an LME. Mechanism: the controlling owner acting to raise capital for the borrower. Authority: Robertshaw. Counter: lender pleads fraud, illegality, or malice. What defeats it: a record that the LME funded the portfolio company.
14. Loans stay outside securities law. Mechanism: syndicated term-loan structure. Authority: Kirschner. Counter: lender pleads the Reves family-resemblance factors. What defeats it: sophisticated lenders and bank-loan documentation.
15. ERISA controlled group. Mechanism: split ownership below 80% across funds with express partnership disclaimers and separate structures. Authority: Sun Capital. Counter: the pension fund pleads a partnership-in-fact under Luna. What defeats it: no shared operating structure between the funds; the trade-or-business question is open.
16. Antitrust roll-ups. Mechanism: reduce to a minority stake with limited board seats to defeat § 13(b) federal suits; expect aggregation of add-ons under Guideline 8. Authority: USAP; Merger Guidelines § 2.8; the Welsh Carson final order as the consent template. Counter: FTC pleads ongoing participation or proceeds administratively. What defeats it: one non-chair seat and no operational role; the portfolio company still carries the structural remedy.
17. HSR filings on the pre-2025 form. Mechanism: file on the reverted form. Authority: FTC PNO page; Chamber v. FTC. Counter: the FTC prevails in the Fifth Circuit (No. 26-40094, in abeyance through Dec. 31, 2026). What defeats it: monitor the appeal; a renewed rulemaking with a cost-benefit record.
18. Securities suits against sponsor-controlled issuers. Mechanism: rebut price impact at certification with an event study and the generic nature of challenged statements; pure omissions are not actionable under 10b-5(b); § 11 requires traceability. Authority: Halliburton II; Goldman Sachs; Macquarie; Slack. Counter: plaintiff identifies a specific half-truth; the issuer bears the burden of persuasion on price impact. What defeats it: an event study showing no back-end price reaction and a mixed registered and unregistered listing record.
19. Texas forum and jury waiver by governing document. Mechanism: designate the Business Court as exclusive forum and waive jury trial in the company agreement or LPA before equity is issued. Authority: §§ 2.115, 2.116. Counter: a holder argues no knowing waiver. What defeats it: (d) deems acquirers after inclusion to have knowingly waived.
20. Texas LLC duty elimination. Mechanism: eliminate manager fiduciary duties in the company agreement. Authority: § 101.401. Counter: § 101.052 limits and contract good-faith claims; for LPs, § 152.006 confines elimination to listed partnerships. What defeats it: express elimination text plus disinterested approval of affiliate deals.
21. Texas presumptions and the independence petition. Mechanism: elect § 21.419 in the charter and petition under § 21.554 for a binding independence finding within 75 days. Authority: §§ 21.419, 21.554. Counter: for corporations under 35 holders, § 21.563 removes the demand and dismissal machinery and permits direct recovery (Sneed). What defeats it: for closely held companies, the shareholders' agreement (Ritchie) rather than the statute.
22. Texas veil shield. Mechanism: § 21.223 bars alter-ego and formality theories absent actual fraud for direct personal benefit. Counter: Keyes routes the claim around the shield as an officer tort. What defeats it: keep sponsor personnel out of officer roles or document every representation they make.
23. Texas interested transactions. Mechanism: disinterested-director approval after disclosure, or a fairness record at authorization. Authority: § 21.418; Poe. Counter: the interested director carries the burden and fairness is measured to the corporation only. What defeats it: contemporaneous price evidence at the approval date.
24. TUFTA exposure on recaps and fees. Mechanism: market-rate fee documentation and solvency analysis at each distribution. Authority: §§ 24.005, 24.010; Janvey. Counter: creditor pleads the insider badge and the one-year discovery tail. What defeats it: four-year repose from payment for constructive claims and an objective market-value record for services.
25. Drag-along enforcement in the Business Court. Mechanism: sole-discretion and duty-limitation language in the LPA. Authority: Primexx. Counter: misapplication of proceeds and sidecar allocation remain triable. What defeats it: a clean allocation waterfall executed as written.
26. Texas health-care acquisitions. Mechanism: no material-change notice or ownership registry applies (S.B. 1595 died in committee); the MSO controls business functions only. Authority: § 164.052; S.B. 1595 history. Counter: Texas Medical Board discipline of physician-owners where the MSO controls clinical practice or fees. What defeats it: physician retention of clinical and fee control in the management agreement.
27. CTA: domestic entities file nothing. Mechanism: treat U.S.-formed sponsor holdcos, blockers, and portfolio companies as outside the reporting-company definition and confine BOI diligence to foreign-formed entities registered in a U.S. state. Authority: 90 FR 13688 (interim final rule, eff. Mar. 26, 2025); 91 FR 52508 (final rule, eff. Aug. 14, 2026). Counterparty's best counter: a blanket CTA-compliance rep for every entity in the chain, or a foreign feeder that registered in a state and never filed. What defeats it: a rep narrowed to foreign-formed registered entities with the final rule's effective date recited, and a filing check on each offshore vehicle's state registrations.
28. HSR: size the add-on against the 2026 threshold, then aggregate. Mechanism: test each platform acquisition and add-on against the size-of-transaction threshold in the current FTC annual notice, re-pulled on the day of signing, never from this file; a sub-threshold add-on files nothing but stays exposed to collective review of the series. Authority: 91 FR 2133 (eff. Feb. 17, 2026); Merger Guidelines § 2.8. Counter: the agency treats a run of sub-threshold add-ons as one anticompetitive pattern under Guideline 8. What defeats it: a dated threshold analysis at signing and a roll-up record that survives review of the series as a whole.
29. Texas LPA drafting after the Primexx full read. Mechanism: pair "sole discretion" with "sole interest" on the drag right, fix a waiting period and require an arm's-length third-party buyer, leave notice and price terms out of the clause if none are to be implied, and disclaim the § 152.204(b)(2) best-interest obligation while defining permitted conduct that does not violate loyalty. Authority: Primexx, 2025 Tex. Bus. 9, ¶¶ 107, 158-160, 170, 179. Counter: the minority LP pleads that the modifications are manifestly unreasonable under § 152.002(b), or shifts to proceeds allocation and the waterfall, which survived summary judgment. What defeats it: a record showing the modifications were never challenged as manifestly unreasonable (the Primexx plaintiffs did not) and a waterfall executed as written; the pending Fifteenth Court appeal (No. 15-25-00120-CV) can move all of this.
30. Texas CPOM: the rule-based route is nonprofit certification, not an MSO. Mechanism: where a sponsor structure needs a lay entity to employ or contract with physicians, the only TMB rule route is a certified § 162.001 non-profit health organization under 22 TAC § 174.2; a for-profit MSO holds business functions only and takes no safe harbor from ch. 174. Authority: 22 TAC §§ 174.1, 174.2, 174.5, 174.10; Occ. Code § 164.052(a)(13), (17). Counter: TMB reads MSO control over clinical practice or fee-setting as the physician-owners aiding unlicensed practice. What defeats it: a management agreement that leaves clinical and fee control with the physicians, and nonprofit certification wherever employment by a lay entity is required.
31. Texas advancement, sponsor as plaintiff against a dissident director-member. Mechanism: plead the drag-along or transfer claim against the resisting holder in his capacity as member, not as director, so the claims are not brought by reason of his service and fall outside a by-reason-of advancement clause; where the target is a director sued for disloyalty, expect to advance once he tenders a written undertaking. Authority: Energy Founders, 2026 Tex. Bus. 17, ¶¶ 42-53; In re Aguilar. Counterparty's best counter: the director-member recasts the pleaded conduct as board conduct, or tenders the § 8.104(a) affirmation and undertaking and seeks mandamus, since Aguilar holds the company's own fiduciary-breach claims, indemnification entitlement, and ability to repay irrelevant to advancement. What defeats it: a live pleading confined to owner transfer obligations with no director-capacity allegations; Energy Founders is trial-level and unreviewed, so the eight-corners framing can move on appeal.
32. Texas advancement drafting for sponsor designees. Mechanism: at a Texas portfolio corporation, make advancement mandatory through the governing documents or an agreement under § 8.104(b), conditioned only on the § 8.104(a) written affirmation and the § 8.104(c) unlimited general undertaking, which need not be secured and may be accepted without regard to ability to repay; file the § 8.152 written report of each advance. Authority: § 8.104(a)-(d). Counter: the company relies on a § 8.003 restriction in the governing documents, or on an ability-to-repay condition of its own making. What defeats it: mandatory text with no board-discretion or ability-to-repay condition, and a documented undertaking tendered before fees accrue; § 8.104(a) alone is permissive.
33. S.B. 29 derivative threshold at a listed or electing platform, after Gusinsky. Mechanism: at an exchange-listed portfolio company or a § 21.419-electing corporation with 500 or more shareholders, adopt the § 21.552(a)(3) ownership-threshold bylaw (capped at three percent) as soon as a derivative demand arrives, because a written demand does not institute a derivative proceeding under S.B. 29 § 26 and a suit filed after adoption is barred. Authority: Gusinsky v. Reynolds, Doc. 52; § 21.552(a)(3); § 21.419. Counter: the holder pleads the bylaw adoption itself as a fiduciary breach, a retroactivity violation under Tex. Const. art. I, § 16, or repleads as direct claims; Gusinsky rejected each on its record, and the ruling is on appeal (notice of appeal Apr. 15, 2026, Doc. 56; no Fifth Circuit disposition located). What defeats it: a threshold at or below three percent, a board record for the adoption, and no derivative suit pending on May 14, 2025; do not cite Gusinsky for the § 21.419 presumption or inspection rights, which it does not address.
